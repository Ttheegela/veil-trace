# Slide guide: "Names change in weeks. The need doesn't."

*A plain-language companion to the 3-minute demo. For each slide: what it says, what you're looking at, the words you might not know, and why it matters. Written for someone who has never worked in sanctions or trade.*

---

## The big picture first

Governments keep **sanctions lists**: names of companies and people that nobody in their country may do business with. Some lists freeze money; others ban selling certain goods, like advanced computer chips, to certain buyers. After Russia's full invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the US, the UK, the EU and others used these lists to cut off Russia's supply of Western chips, which its military needs for drones, missiles and radios.

The lists have one weak spot: **they are lists of names.** A company can be replaced by a new one with a different name in a few weeks. Getting a new name onto a list takes investigators, lawyers and paperwork: more than a year, as we show. Our demo follows the chips through trade records to show that gap, and argues for watching what *doesn't* change.

---

## Slide 1. The question

**What it says:** A sanctions check matches names, so a company with a new name gets through.

**What you're looking at:** A simple flow. A compliance officer checks each order against the government list. An order from a listed company stops. An order from a new company, which is really the same buyer behind a new name, ships, because the new name isn't on the list yet.

**Words you might not know**
- **Compliance officer:** the person inside a company whose job is to make sure it follows the law, including sanctions. At a chip distributor they decide whether an order may ship.
- **Chip distributor:** a company that buys chips in bulk from the makers (like Texas Instruments or STMicroelectronics) and resells them to thousands of smaller buyers. Most chips reach their final user through distributors, not directly from the maker.
- **Screening:** checking a customer's name against the lists before doing business. Usually automated.

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** the whole system depends on this one check working. If it can be beaten by changing a name, the sanctions leak.
- **For a company:** a distributor that ships to the wrong buyer can face large fines and lose its right to export, even if it screened the name correctly.

## Slide 2. New routes

**What it says:** After Russia invaded Ukraine, computer chips kept reaching Russia, sent through countries that had never sent them before.

**What you're looking at:** A timeline, 2019 to 2024. Grey bars are long-time senders (Germany, Turkey). Blue bars are countries that only start sending chips to Russia in April and May 2022, weeks after the invasion: **Kyrgyzstan (1,147 shipments), the UAE (2,041) and Kazakhstan (715)**. Then a second source: Kyrgyzstan's own export reports go from almost nothing to **$13.5 million** of chips to Russia in 2023.

**Words you might not know**
- **Customs records:** the paperwork every shipment crosses a border with: what it is, who sent it, who receives it, where it came from. Our main data here is Russia's own import records, collected by the data company **Sayari**.
- **Declared country:** the country the paperwork *says* the goods came from. It isn't a GPS trace; goods can be relabelled along the way.
- **Transshipment:** sending goods through a third country so they don't look like they're going straight to the real destination.
- **UN Comtrade:** a United Nations database of what each country reports it exported. A second, independent source.
- **HS 8542:** the international customs code for chips (integrated circuits).

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** sanctions didn't stop the chips; they rerouted them. Small economies next to Russia suddenly became chip exporters, though almost none of the chips were made there (only 45 of Kyrgyzstan's 1,147 shipments say "made in Kyrgyzstan").
- **For a company:** an order headed to one of these new transit countries deserves a second look, even when the buyer's name is clean.

## Slide 3. New names

**What it says:** The shipping companies change every few weeks. The flow of chips, and the buyer at the end, stay the same.

**What you're looking at:** A "river" chart of one route, Kyrgyzstan into Russia. The blue line is the monthly flow of chip shipments. Each band underneath is a shipping company. They light up one after another: 1, then 4, then 12, then 6 companies per half-year, while the flow keeps going. A typical company was seen in the records for only **40 days**. Meanwhile one Russian buyer appears from **2019 to 2025**, while its suppliers change underneath it.

**Words you might not know**
- **Shipper:** the company named on the paperwork as sending the goods.
- **Front company (shell company):** a company that exists mainly on paper, often newly registered, used to put a clean name on a transaction. Several of these firms were registered within weeks of the invasion (one 18 days after it).
- **Buyer:** the company in Russia receiving the goods.

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** listing the shipping companies one by one is like arresting couriers while the customer keeps ordering. The thing that persists, the buyer, is the better target.
- **For a company:** a customer you've never heard of, set up recently, selling into a sensitive market, is the pattern to flag, even before any list names it.

## Slide 4. The lag

**What it says:** Lists arrive late. By the time a firm is listed, it has usually moved on.

**What you're looking at:** Four companies from these routes. The dark line under each is its shipment records. The blue bar runs from when the company first appeared (registered, or first shipped) to the day a government first listed it: **at least 15 to 20 months**. **4 of 5** listed shipping firms on these routes had already left them by the day they were listed. One, **RM Design and Development**, kept shipping to Russia after the US sanctioned it: **40 records**, declared as leaving Turkey.

**Words you might not know**
- **Listed / designated:** officially added to a sanctions or export-control list.
- **OFAC:** the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control, which keeps the main US sanctions list.
- **Entity List:** a separate US Commerce Department list of companies that need a special licence before anyone may sell them US-made goods. For one firm here (ELEM Group) it came before the Treasury listing.
- **Hatched area:** Russia's records thin out after late 2023, so "stopped" there may partly mean "the data stopped". We shade it to say so.

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** a 15 to 20 month delay means a front company can do most of its business before it is ever named. By then the flow has moved to a new name.
- **For a company:** a clean screening result means "not listed *yet*", not "safe". Checking the name once is not enough.

## Slide 5. The drone part

**What it says:** The exact chip model found in a Russian attack drone was shipped into Russia months before any company on its path was listed. Whether these particular chips reached a weapon is not proven.

**What you're looking at:** On the left, a Shahed-136 drone. Ukraine's defence intelligence has documented a specific chip model inside it. We searched shipment records for that exact part number and found it: **100 pieces in August 2022 and 22 more in November 2022**, from two Hong Kong companies to one Russian buyer. Every company on that path was listed **11 to 17 months** later. The dashed line between the drone and the chip says **"Not proven"**, and it stays on screen.

**Words you might not know**
- **Shahed-136:** an Iranian-designed attack drone used by Russia against Ukraine.
- **Part number:** the maker's exact code for one chip model. Unlike a company name, it doesn't change.
- **GUR:** Ukraine's defence intelligence agency. It publishes the foreign parts it finds in Russian weapons. It is a party to the war, so we cite it as one.
- **Dual-use goods:** everyday items (chips, sensors, radios) that also have military uses.

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** ordinary commercial chips, sold through ordinary distributors, end up in weapons. Following the part number is one way to see the flow.
- **For a company:** the maker and its distributors are the start of this chain. Knowing which of your parts are on the "high priority" list for weapons, and watching where those part numbers go, is now part of the job.

## Slide 6. Blind spots

**What it says:** No single source sees everything: not our data, and not any one screening tool.

**What you're looking at:** A map. Hatched countries (such as Kyrgyzstan, Armenia and Hong Kong) keep no trade records of their own in our data; we only see them through their partners' records. **81%** of the chip records into Russia come from Russia's own customs data, which thins out after late 2023. Then a real catch: a Dubai chip shipper went on the US sanctions list on **12 June 2024**, but a data provider's flag still called it clean. Our own checker caught it.

**Words you might not know**
- **Data provider:** a company that collects and cleans trade and company records and sells access. Our two, **Sayari** (shipment records, company owners) and **Tradeverifyd** (company-to-company trade links, dated sanctions flags), checked each other.
- **Flag:** a provider's label saying a company is sanctioned. Flags can be wrong or out of date.
- **Skeptic / verifier:** a step in our team that re-derived every number from the saved source data. It caught doubled totals, a wrong date and the clean-flagged shipper.

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** investigators and journalists often rely on one database. Its gaps become everyone's gaps.
- **For a company:** relying on one screening tool is a real risk. We found listed firms that one tool marked clean and another tool couldn't find at all. Using two sources that check each other is cheap insurance.

---

## Slide 7. What to track

**What it says:** Track what doesn't change: the product, the buyer, the route.

**What you're looking at:** A time scale from weeks to years. A company name lasts weeks. The route lasts months (April 2022 through 2023). The buyer lasts years (2019 to 2025). Lists arrive 15 to 20 months after a company appears, long after the name has changed, but while the product, the buyer and the route are still there.

**Why it matters**
- **For the world:** enforcement that follows the persistent parts of the chain (buyers, part numbers, routes, and for ships the hull number) can move faster than lists of names.
- **For a company:** screen the buyer and the part, not just the seller's name. Ask: is this part on the high-priority list? Is this route new? Is this buyer's supplier new?

---

## Where the data comes from

- **Sayari:** shipment records (mostly Russia's own import records), company and ownership records, watchlists.
- **Tradeverifyd:** company-to-company trade links and dated sanctions flags.
- **UN Comtrade:** countries' own export reports.
- **Official lists:** US Treasury (OFAC) and Commerce (Entity List), UK and EU.
- **Ukraine's GUR** weapon-components database (a party to the war).

Every number on the slides opens its source when you press **E** during the demo. Documented conduct is stated plainly; where something is our own inference, we call it a lead.
